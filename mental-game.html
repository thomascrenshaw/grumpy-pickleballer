🥒 The Mental Game

The in-match head game: the serve routine, the reset, and the silent place you go between points.

My serve is one of the few things I can do with my eyes closed. Which doesn't mean I should — but it's what I was aiming for.

First, the honest disclaimer: this is my routine, about a year into playing. Yours might look nothing like it — you may read the next few paragraphs and think "what in the hell is this guy doing." Fair. The specific moves don't transfer; the reason for having them does. Because once you've got the basics of the game down — and that bar is lower than you'd think — pickleball is almost entirely mental, a game you play against yourself far more than against the people across the net.

Here's the cool version of me: a hard top-spin serve so grooved that the drop, the timing, and the contact are automatic — eyes shut, still deep, still heavy. That version is real and it took a lot of reps to build. But it isn't the useful part. The useful part is what got me there, and it's boring on purpose: a routine. Not to make me a machine — you can't be a machine on a pickleball court, and half this page is about why you shouldn't try — but to make me consistent. Those are different goals, and mixing them up is where a lot of nervous serves go to die.

The routine is a kit, not a checklist

Most pre-serve-routine advice hands you a numbered list to run in order, every single time. Mine isn't like that. It's a small pile of pieces, and as long as I hit some of them, the routine did its job. There's no required order — because only one piece actually matters, and the rest are just there to walk me toward it. That's the whole reframe: a routine isn't a ritual you have to complete correctly. It's a set of on-ramps to one mental state, and you only need to find one on-ramp.

The pieces

So here's the pile, roughly in the order it tends to happen — but don't read that as a sequence. The point ends and I tap my partner's paddle. Good point or bad, it's the same tap and it means the same thing: that one's gone, wipe it away, it is never coming back. If the other side earned it, I say so — great angle, great hit, good rally, whatever's true. Then I walk to the back of the court and tap my paddle on whatever's there. Back fence, divider, a tree stump — doesn't matter. The tap is the period at the end of the last point.

Then the ball. I bounce the ball off the ground and up into my left hand — which makes a certain quiet noise I've come to find calming — close my eyes, and take a breath. I look once at where the returner is standing, clock where their partner is, make sure mine is ready, and then I don't look up again. I call the score, usually botch it, and it doesn't matter in the slightest, because the train has left the station and the ball is already coming. I bend at the waist, switch my grip if I'm hitting anything other than top-spin, and hit it. And the instant I do, it's go time.

The one that matters

Out of that whole pile, exactly one piece is load-bearing: closing my eyes and taking the breath. Everything else is an on-ramp — calming, habitual, nice to have, and completely skippable. The breath is where the noise stops. Fumble every other piece but land that one and I'm fine; nail all of them but skip that one and I'm serving distracted. That's why the order doesn't matter and finishing the "checklist" doesn't matter. The routine has one job — hand me off to the quiet — and any on-ramp that gets me there did the job.

The game within the game. There's exactly one way to truly blow the routine, and it's not mental — it's the ball. Every so often your hand just forgets what it's holding, the ball squirts loose, you fumble it, then you kick it reaching down, all while the other three players quietly lose it — because every one of them has been there. Feature, not a bug. Same family as four grown adults forgetting the score one rally later. The plastic orb humbles everybody eventually. Laugh, pick it up, restart the routine.

Consistent, not a machine

When the routine's working, a big chunk of the time I land the serve within a foot of the baseline — deep, heavy, hard to attack. Consistently, which I'm told is the fancy word for it, though I've never been sure who "they" are in that sentence. Something about the timing, the grip pressure, the swing speed, the paddle angle, and the follow-through is just dialed in.

But not always — and here's the important part: not always is fine. Some come up short. Sometimes the follow-through dies or the face is a hair off and the ball goes somewhere I didn't ask for. That's not the routine failing. That's being a human and not a machine. You will never hit your serve exactly where you want it 100% of the time in a real match — there are too many variables crammed into that half-second. What you can do is make it consistent, and consistency comes from the routine, not from squeezing the grip harder and wanting it more.

There's even a part of me that refuses to take it fully seriously. I still creep in when I shouldn't. I still forget to let it bounce every now and then. Some corner of my brain decides to freelance without checking with me first, and I've made peace with it. That's the humanity — and the humanity is the reason you're out here in the first place.

When you miss, there's a reason

So when you miss — and you will — don't just shrug it off, and don't let your partner's reaction, or the version of it you're imagining, run you. Your partner shouldn't care that you dumped one into the net, so neither should you. What you should do instead is replay the shot. Stop before you move if you have to, and actually look at it: where were your feet, where was your paddle, how far were you from the net, from the baseline. Because you've hit that exact shot cleanly hundreds of times — over the net, in the court, point won. So why was this one different?

My theory is that your head was somewhere else. Maybe you were already admiring the wide-open court they left you to tap the ball into. Maybe your shoe's too tight. Either way, instead of the soft ball into the open space, you rifled it into the net — the shot was physical, but the miss was mental. So learn it, file it away, catalog it. That cataloging loop is where a lot of the actual getting better happens, and it's the whole reason I keep saying the mental game is the game the moment you're past the basics.

Between points: close your eyes, wait for the sound

The rest of the time — when I'm not the one serving — the routine strips down to almost nothing, and it's built on the same move: close my eyes and wait for a sound. If we're serving and my partner has the ball, I tap whatever's behind me, close my eyes, and wait for the crack of the ball off their paddle. Closing my eyes to focus is a lifelong thing — building a bike, trying to start a finicky bolt into a hole I can't quite see, I close my eyes. It's just how my hands find their attention.

If we're receiving and I'm not the returner, I look at the ground until I hear the ball come off the paddle. Either way that sound is the trigger: eyes up, locked on the ball, go time. If I'm the one up at the kitchen line, I'm already in a continental grip so I can push a drive right back. And if I am the returner, it's almost the serve routine in reverse — same quiet, one difference: I keep my eyes on the server the whole time, reading how the ball leaves their paddle.

Watch the top pros while they wait on a serve. Ben Johns looks like he's waiting for a bus: loose, eyes down, then up at the server, nothing wasted. The second the ball is struck, he's all the way on. That's the whole trick. Calm isn't the absence of intensity; it's where you keep it until you need it.

The silent head

Here's the crux of the whole thing, and it's less a technique than a place I go. When I'm on a pickleball court playing a match, I am playing pickleball. That's the entire list. Not work, not the drive home, not the last point, not the score I just butchered calling out. People have genuinely asked me between points if I'm okay, because I've gone somewhere and I'm not tracking the chatter around me. I'm fine. I'm better than fine — I'm in the one place my brain actually shuts up.

It is the quietest my head ever gets. Nothing exists but the sound of the ball hitting the paddle. I come out of one zone at the end of a point and drop straight into the next one, and that hand-off — under all the paddle taps and breaths and grip changes — is the actual ritual. Get to the silence. Let the sound of the ball be the only thing allowed in.
