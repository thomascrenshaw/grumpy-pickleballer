🥒 Why You Play

Not how to win — why you're out here, how you treat people, and the one rule you never break.

I don't care about the score — it just tells me who's serving.

That's not a humblebrag and it's not zen. It's the only sane way to play a game that's rigged to make you look bad. The scoreboard tracks exactly one thing: whose turn it is to serve. It says nothing about whether you're playing well, whether you belong out here, or whose fault the last shot was. The second you let a number in a little window decide how you feel, you've lost the point before it's even served. Keep the score for serving. Keep your head for playing.

Nobody can remember it anyway

Here's the tell. Watch four grown adults try to hold a score for one rally. It gets called out clear as day — 6-4-2 — somebody serves, the ball's dead four seconds later, and all four players look at each other like they just surfaced from a nap. Whose serve? What was it? This happens every single game, it's one of the funniest recurring bugs in the human operating system, and it's the whole argument in miniature: if that number mattered as much as your nervous system thinks it does, you'd be able to hang onto it for more than one rally. You can't. Nobody can. So maybe hold it a little looser.

Side quest: if the score is this forgettable, maybe the scoring's the problem, not us. Rally scoring — a point to whoever wins the rally, every rally, no tracking which server you are — collapses the whole thing to one number that ticks every time. It's creeping into rec play for exactly this reason. A rant for another day.

The game is built for you to fail

And that's before you remember the whole thing is rigged against you. The court's too small to hit through. The paddle's too hot, so your "controlled" drive sails long. The ball is a hollow plastic sphere full of holes that won't bounce the same way twice — and the moment there's a breeze, it does something nobody predicted, including the wind. You are playing a game engineered from the equipment up to produce errors. Once that really lands, something in your shoulders drops. You stop taking the misses personally, because the misses are the design spec. The absurdity isn't ruining your day — it's the joke you signed up for. And you signed up for it to get out of your house, onto a court, into the air, with people you actually like.

You win by outlasting, not out-hitting

If the game punishes big swings, stop taking them. This is the head-side of the reset: patience is a weapon, not a white flag. Get the ball back over one more time than they'd like, and make them be the one who has to manufacture a winner out of nothing — because manufacturing winners is exactly where errors come from. The player willing to hit one more boring, unattackable ball beats the player trying to end the point almost every time. You don't have to be better than them. You have to out-patient them by one shot.

Play the person, not your ego

Winning and dominating are not the same thing, and mixing them up is how a good player turns into the partner nobody wants, or the one who quietly stops getting invited back. Teeing off on a beginner with everything you own isn't competitiveness — it's ego in a competitiveness costume. You already know you can hit hard; landing it on someone two levels down proves nothing to anyone. Make them earn the point instead of ripping it away from them. That's the harder game, and the better one.

Playing down is a gift, not an insult

Playing down doesn't mean playing down to someone. Just the opposite. Feed the less-experienced player some genuinely tough shots — ones they have to stretch for, the kind that leave them a little better at the end of the session than they were at the start. You're out here in your 30s, 40s, 50s, 60s, 70s, 80s playing a game you love, and somebody either helped you get to this point or you sure wish they had. Pay this shit forward. You'll get to play plenty of other days, at whatever intensity you want. This game, right now, can be the one where somebody levels up because they got to hit against you.

Two hard rules while you do it. Don't mansplain how to hit it softer, harder, or more accurately. And don't coach — at all — unless you've asked them whether they'd even like input. Unsolicited coaching isn't generosity. It's just one more way to make the game about you.

Take another one

When a serve finds the net in a crew game, the regular group you play with week in and week out, I'll usually say "take another one." That isn't charity, and it's never a pity do-over. The server gets another rep at the serve, the receiver gets another rep at the return, and that's two people getting better instead of one point getting scored. A mulligan is practice, handed out to everybody on the court, me included.

The same logic runs the other way: serve to the person in front of you. I've got a deep top-spin serve I'm genuinely proud of, and there's no reason to pull it out on a friendly Tuesday against someone who can't handle it yet. All it produces is a point. Nobody rallies, nobody learns, and nobody on the court really benefits, including me. That doesn't mean serve them a softball. Give them something they have to work to return but can return, and then let the rally be the hard part.

And when it happens to you: if someone drops a tight-angle ace on you in a crew game, it almost certainly wasn't a flex. More likely the ball came off the paddle a little differently than they meant it to, which happens to all of us, and it happened to land somewhere unreachable. Chuckle about it, tell them "nice serve" and mean it, then step into your between-point routine — the pile of pieces where only one of them really matters — and get ready for the next one. If you fumble the ball on the way, welcome to the game within the game.
A point nobody got to play doesn't teach anybody anything.

Let it go between points

You can't get the last point back. It's gone whether you stew on it or not, which makes stewing pure cost and zero refund. And be honest about who the opponent actually is out here. The ball isn't the enemy — it's a plastic ball. The people across the net aren't the enemy — they're your friends, or they're about to be. The only real enemy on the court is the voice in your own skull relitigating a point that's already over. Quiet that voice and the whole game opens up.

So come play however you feel like playing today — hard, soft, goofy, laser-focused, whatever you brought. Same rule as the rest of life: don't let your way of having fun get in the way of somebody else having theirs.

Tap the paddle

This one supposedly goes without saying, which is exactly why it has to be said — because people still blow it. When the game's over, no matter how ugly it got, no matter how sideways your mood — you look your partner — especially your partner, this part is critical — and your opponents in the eye, you say "good game," "fun game," "nice game," whatever works for you, and you tap their fucking paddle. Every time. Non-negotiable.

Your bad day is nobody's fault but your own. It happens to all of us and it isn't a crime — but it doesn't get to leak onto the three people who just spent twenty minutes on the court with you. And here's the line that ties this whole page together: your partner probably won't remember the score by the time they reach their car. They will absolutely remember whether you were a good sport. That's the one stat that follows you — to the next game, the next group, the next open play. The person who can't be bothered to tap paddles after a rough one just told you exactly what they'd be like as a partner. Believe them.

Tap the paddle. Let it go. Come back next week.
