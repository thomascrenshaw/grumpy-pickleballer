🥒 Getting Better

No hack — it's reps. The learning ladder, the out ball, and the hardest skill nobody names: going against your own brain.

Improving takes time. The more you play and rep, the better you get. That's the whole secret — and it's a lousy one, because you were hoping for a hack.

There's no shortcut and I'm not going to invent one for you. You get better by playing more and repping more, full stop. But "better" is a slippery word, so start by asking: better than whom? Because if the honest answer is that you already beat everyone in your regular group — congratulations, you now have a job.

If you're the best in the group, that's a job

Your job is to make the group better, and the way you do that is not by coaching or lecturing — we covered over on Why You Play why unsolicited advice is really just a way of making the game about you. You do it by playing down only to the exact point where you're still challenging them. Not one notch further. If you make it easy, you're not being nice — you're disrespecting them and their desire to get better. A friend and mentor put it to me in exactly those words, and he's right: dialing it all the way down tells people you don't think they're worth a real ball. Give them shots they have to reach for. That's the gift.

The ladder: five steps

Here's how I break down learning a new shot while I'm actually playing games. Say somebody better than me keeps catching me on angles I can't handle yet.

  1. Make the effort.This is the hardest part.Sharp angle, lob over your partner's head: run for it.
  2. Get paddle on it.This is the hardest part.That's progress. If you're touching it, you're reading it earlier and reacting faster than you were. A minute ago you were whiffing it entirely; now your brain has started to solve where the ball is going. You're anticipating. Hang onto that word.
  3. Get it over, and in.This is the hardest part.ATP or OTN (over the net), and landing inside the lines. Pointing the paddle face the right way while you're moving is hard. Loosening your grip right before contact while you're moving is hard.
  4. Put it where you want it.This is the hardest part.Now it's a game of a couple of degrees of paddle face and a couple percent of power. Minor tweaks, major rewards.
  5. Stop thinking about it.This is the hardest part.For me, it was also the easiest. Once I'm confident I can do something repeatably (the serve, the return, the lob, chasing down a lob), it's autopilot.
It's all the hardest part, because the hardest part is always the thing you're learning. The one that takes the longest is placement. Always will be.
The around-the-post party. Want a shot you get to actively celebrate? Learn the ATP. Grab a friend and practice curling a wide dink around the post onto the far court — and mind the specific court, because every one has different side posts and obstructions. Don't sprint full-speed into the fence chasing it; the fence will not move for you, no matter how committed you are. At most levels an ATP is basically a small party: you celebrate the shot, not the point, and everyone around you is thrilled because it's genuinely hard. And when mine clangs off the post instead? I switch the paddle to my left hand, run my right hand through my hair, and say "psyche." Then on to the next point. I still have to consciously think one through in the half-second before the second bounce — which means that shot isn't on step five for me yet.

Step five, up close

The last step of learning anything is to stop thinking about it. This is the serve routine again: I'm not thinking about my serve, I just know it's golden — unless it isn't, because this is pickleball and the game is designed to make you fail. When a ball is rocketing at my face, I don't think "I should get my paddle up and block this." I just do it. That's the top of the ladder: effort, paddle on ball, over and in, placement, then reps until the shot drops out of your conscious brain entirely and your body just handles it. You can't jump to this step. You rep your way up to it — same as the serve you can hit with your eyes closed.

The out ball, and the lie your brain tells

Now the hardest thing in the game, which isn't a shot at all — it's a decision. There is no worse feeling than calling "No!" on a ball and then watching it drop a foot inside the baseline. And here's what your brain does next: the very next time, it makes you hit a ball that was sailing out — and you'll never know it was out, because you hit it.

That's flawed wiring, and it's the exact same bug as the guy at the roulette wheel who watches red hit fifteen times and shoves his chips onto black — because surely it can't be red again. It can. The wheel has no memory, and neither does the ball. Getting burned last time tells you nothing about this time. There's a solid chance that whatever you choose — let it fly or take a whack — will be wrong, and that's just the nature of the game.

Letting the ball go is, for real, one of the hardest skills in pickleball to learn — there are whole videos on it. You're reading trajectory and speed while your brain also tries to account for spin, wind direction, how hard it was hit, what paddle hit it, how reliably that player actually finds the line… it's an impossible pile of math for the time you've got. So here's my actual advice: do what you think is right and don't second-guess it. Don't stand there afterward muttering "that was probably going out," because you have no way of knowing. The one exception is obvious — if you're reaching outside the sideline or way behind the baseline to make contact, yeah, that one was going out, put your paddle down. But standing at the kitchen line? Your guess is exactly as good as everyone else's, which is precisely why all four of you just stood there and watched it sail past.

Luck cuts both ways

One rule I actually hold, and it keeps me honest: if a good shot was "just luck," then a bad one was just luck too. You don't get to summon the wind only when it's against you — crediting every winner to your own skill and charging every error to fate. Own both or own neither. And mostly? Own neither, take the note, and move on — because nine times out of ten the variable wasn't luck at all. It was your paddle, and your paddle takes instruction.

Going against the grain of your brain

So here's the whole ladder, start to finish: get your paddle on the ball, hit it with the sweet spot, get it over the net, dial it in through reps. And carry the one hard truth with you — the only ball that definitely goes out is the one you let by, and even that isn't a sure thing.

Here's what makes all of it so hard, and it's kind of beautiful. You have less than a second to make most decisions on a pickleball court. Most of us can't decide what to have for dinner without convening a family meeting. So getting better at pickleball isn't really about pickleball. What you're actually doing, rep after rep after rep, is teaching yourself to go against the grain of your own brain.

You're not really learning pickleball. You're teaching yourself to go against the grain of your own brain.
